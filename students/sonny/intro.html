Introduction

abstract

Following the implementation of Taiwan’s landmark "no-kill" policy in 2013, public animal shelters have faced capacity strains due to a structural imbalance between stray intake and adoption rates. This study investigates the efficacy of financial incentives as a policy tool to promote public demand for canine adoption. Utilizing a general census survey methodology administered to the general public, we evaluate general attitudes toward adoption when paired with economic subsidies. The empirical results indicate that while financial incentives do not elicit a radical shift towards willingness, they produce a statistically positive, incremental "nudge" in public inclination. These findings suggest that while financial subsidies may not serve as a guaranteed fix, they function as an effective catalyst for changes in attitudes towards adoption. Consequently, integrating economic incentives into public animal welfare frameworks offers a viable strategy to mitigate the shelter overcrowding crisis.

Predictions


Q1 Prediction We expect a low response rate for Q1. Because financial help for dog adoption is a specific topic that people have to actively look up, a general lack of public awareness will likely limit participation. Q2 Prediction We expect Q2 to get more answers and a wider variety of responses. Because it includes all ways of getting a dog (including buying one), it applies to more people, especially owners with personal experience. Q3 & Q5 Prediction While people will likely agree with both ideas about financial help, support for Q5 (direct money) will be higher than for Q3 (long-term planning). This is because people usually prefer quick, clear rewards. Q4 Prediction We expect Q4 to split people into two clear, opposite groups based on whether they own a pet. Dog owners will likely disagree because they understand the personal costs and risks, while non-owners will likely agree because it does not affect them personally.
