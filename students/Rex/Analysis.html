Analysis

Data Analysis

In question 1, we asked 51 people if they know any financial support for adopting a dog, and only one person clearly answered that they are aware of such support.

For question 2, “Please name any financial support you know of for dog ownership in general,” the result was slightly better, with around 5 to 6 people able to give an answer. However, this number is still very small, showing that most people are not familiar with any financial assistance programs related to dog adoption.

For Question 3, which asked whether financial planning support would increase respondents’ willingness to adopt a dog, the majority of participants gave positive responses, such as “Agree” or “Yes 1/2.” This indicates that financial concerns are an important factor when people consider adopting a dog. Many potential adopters may worry about the long-term costs of pet ownership, including food, medical care, grooming, and other unexpected expenses. Even though many respondents may not be aware of the support programs that already exist, the results suggest that providing financial planning advice could make adoption seem more affordable and manageable. Therefore, offering financial guidance or financial assistance programs may encourage more people to adopt dogs from shelters and help increase overall adoption rates.

In question 4, opinions about increasing taxes on breeders and pet shops were more divided. Most people disagree, with overall 33% disagreeing, and with 29.4%agreeing. Also 25% of respondents were neutral, with no opinion. This is important because these responses show that people have different ideas, some think it could help increase the number of dog adoptions.

In Question 5, respondents were asked whether better vet care would increase their willingness to adopt a dog.Most respondents agreed or strongly agreed with this statement, showing strong support for improving animal healthcare services. Only a small number of people disagreed, indicating that vet care is an important consideration for potential adopters. This is important because many people may be concerned about the health and medical costs of owning a dog. Better vet care can make adoption seem more reliable and less stressful. Overall, the results suggest that improving vet services could increase adoption willingness and help encourage more people to adopt dogs from shelters.

Overall, the data shows that awareness of financial support is very low, but people are generally more willing to adopt if financial assistance or better animal care systems are provided. However, opinions are more mixed when it comes to policy changes such as increasing taxes on breeders.

Conclusion

Conclusion
The data collected in this study does not suggest that the public is indifferent to dog adoption. It suggests something different, and therefore more actionable: people are willing, but they are not supported. That distinction matters for what comes next.

Three findings, taken together, point toward a possible direction for future action. First, 98% of respondents had no awareness of any existing financial support for adopted dogs. Second, 82% said that improved access to veterinary care would make them more likely to adopt. Third, only 49% said the same about financial planning support from rescue centres. The gap between those last two numbers represents something specific. People are not deterred from adoption by abstract financial uncertainty. They are deterred by the specific, unpredictable, and potentially catastrophic cost of emergency veterinary care. Any future intervention that does not address that directly could be addressing a problem that most people care less about.

Expand Veterinary Access for Adopted Dogs
The single most supported action in this study is improving veterinary care options for adopted dogs. Future efforts should focus on establishing formal partnerships between rescue centres and veterinary clinics, subsidised first-year health packages for adopted animals, reduced consultation fees for registered adopters, or access to low-cost emergency care funds. This does not require new government policy to begin. Rescue organisations can negotiate these partnerships directly, and the data suggests this would be the most effective use of limited resources. Of all the measures tested in this survey, this is the one with the broadest cross-demographic support as it resonated across all age groups and both genders without meaningful resistance.

Develop Financial Planning Resources at Rescue Centres
Roughly half of respondents said they would be more inclined to adopt if rescue centres offered financial planning support. That is not a majority, but it isn’t a number to dismiss. It represents a meaningful portion of prospective adopters who feel financially unprepared and would benefit from structured guidance. Future action should include the development of clear, accessible cost breakdowns that rescue centres can provide at the point of adoption: estimated annual food costs, typical veterinary expenses, licensing fees, and information about any subsidy schemes available. This does not need to be a formal financial advisory service. A well-designed one-page resource, honestly written, would be a significant improvement over what currently exists.

Target the 35–44 Age Group in Adoption Campaigns
The data identified 35–44 year-olds as the demographic most likely to be influenced by both financial planning support and improved veterinary care, with 73% and 100% favourable rates respectively. This group, which is more likely in stable employment and potentially with families, represents the highest-value audience for adoption outreach. Future campaigns should be designed with this cohort in mind, addressing the practical concerns of established adults rather than relying on emotional appeals aimed at a general audience. This does not mean other age groups should be ignored. It means that limited campaign budgets would produce the most measurable returns if they began here. It is also important to say that the demographic below the age of 18 is most likely to be directly affected by this group, as they have typically less say in whether or not to own a dog.
Reconsider the Breeder Tax as a Policy Approach

This study found no majority support for increasing taxes on dogs purchased from breeders or pet shops. Opinion was almost evenly split: 37% in favour, 39% opposed, 24% neutral. The gender divide on this question was the sharpest demographic difference in the entire dataset. It could be suggested that future policy efforts focus on positive incentives rather than punitive measures, at least in the near term. Not because a breeder tax is necessarily wrong, but because policy that lacks public legitimacy is difficult to implement and easy to oppose. Building a strong adoption infrastructure first with accessible vet care, visible financial support, and effective public communication creates the conditions in which a conversation about breeder regulation becomes more productive.

Conduct Further Research on Unrepresented Groups
In the beginning it was expected that two different groups would exist, one which understood or was aware of dog-related financial support, and one which was not aware of any. However, as the research shows, it is almost unanimous that people do not know of any. This means that people who have actual knowledge about the research subject are practically unrepresented, and therefore trends are unable to be found within people who do know of dog-related financial support. A possible solution for this is to specifically ask people who work in animal related sectors in hope that they would know more about this, and that certain trends can be discovered.
